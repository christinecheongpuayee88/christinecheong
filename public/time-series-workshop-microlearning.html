Why do we need a forecast? What must be forecast? How should we build and trust it?

Three short blocks, eight guided decisions. Each step: a question, a quick explanation, a real example, your decision, and immediate feedback.

WHY→ WHAT→ HOW
BLOCK 1 · WHY

Clarify the decision

Essential question: Why is analysis needed, and is forecasting the correct analytical intent?

Microlearning 1.1 · Start with the business decision

What decision does this forecast actually need to support?

Before touching any data, name the decision. Learners identify: what decision must be made, who will use the result, when action must be taken, and what over- or under-forecasting costs.

Example — The hotel must decide how many part-time staff to schedule and how much inventory to order.
Your turn — complete the decision statement
Model answer: "We need the forecast to support staffing and inventory decisions by predicting monthly demand 12 months ahead." There's no single right answer — the test is whether your statement names a decision, an owner, and a timeframe.
Microlearning 1.2 · Identify the analytics intent

"What will monthly hotel occupancy be over the coming year?" — which type of question is this?

Not every business question is a forecasting question. Match the question to the right analytics intent before choosing a method.

flowchart LR
  A[Business decision] --> B{Analytics intent?}
  B -->|Who or which case?| C[Predictive analytics]
  B -->|How will values evolve?| D[Forecasting analytics]
  B -->|What if we intervene?| E[Causal analytics]
Analytics intentMain questionExample
PredictiveWhich cases are likely to experience an outcome?Which customers may churn?
ForecastingHow will a measure evolve over time?What will monthly occupancy be?
CausalWhat happens if we take an action?Did the promotion increase sales?
Which analytics intent fits?
Correct pathway: Forecasting analytics. The question asks how a measure (occupancy) evolves over time — the defining trait of a forecasting question, not a predictive (who?) or causal (what if?) one.
Go deeper
Pathway to hold onto for the rest of this workshop: Question → intuition → visual → learner decision → feedback. You'll repeat this loop in every unit ahead.
Block output

A decision statement — the sentence you'll carry into Block 2.

Duration: 10–15 minutes
Before you continue — typical emphasis, not a fixed mapping. It's tempting to read Predictive → operational, Forecasting → tactical, Causal → strategic as a rule. It isn't — all three analytics types can support all three decision levels.

BLOCK 2 · WHAT

Define the forecast

Essential question: What exactly must be forecast, over what horizon, and with what evidence?

Microlearning 2.1 · Specify the forecast requirement

Can you name all five elements of a forecast requirement?

Target, granularity, frequency, horizon, and decision — miss one and the forecast won't fit the decision it's meant to support.

ElementQuestion
1. TargetWhat measure?
2. GranularityBy product, store, market or total?
3. FrequencyHourly, daily, weekly, monthly or quarterly?
4. HorizonHow far ahead?
5. DecisionWhat will change because of the forecast?

Example — Forecast monthly occupied hotel rooms by property for the next 12 months to support staffing and supply planning.

Your turn — specify your own forecast requirement
Check yourself against the hotel example: Target = occupied rooms, Granularity = by property, Frequency = monthly, Horizon = 12 months, Decision = staffing and supply planning. If any of your five is missing, the forecast will be hard to act on.

Answer Microlearning 1.2 above with "Forecasting analytics" to unlock this decision.

Microlearning 2.2 · Determine the forecast horizon

Monthly occupancy, forecast 12 months out, to plan staffing and inventory — which horizon is this?

The horizon must be interpreted relative to the data frequency and decision cycle, not just the raw number of periods.

flowchart LR
  A[Forecasting analytics] --> B{Forecast horizon?}
  B -->|Short| C[Operational planning]
  B -->|Medium| D[Tactical planning]
  B -->|Long| E[Strategic scenarios]
HorizonPractical meaningTypical decisions
ShortNext few periods — recent patterns and shocksStaffing, replenishment
Medium~ one seasonal cycle — seasonality and external driversCapacity, inventory, budgeting
LongBeyond one seasonal cycle — structural assumptions, uncertainty and scenariosInvestment, strategic planning
Which horizon fits the hotel scenario?
Medium horizon. Twelve months of monthly data is roughly one full seasonal cycle — the regime where trend, seasonality, and known drivers matter most, and where SARIMA/SARIMAX-style models tend to earn their keep.

BLOCK 3 · HOW

Select, build and validate

Essential question: Which forecasting method is appropriate, and can its forecast be trusted?

Locked

Answer Microlearning 2.2 above with "Medium" to unlock the model-selection tree.

Microlearning 3.1 · Select the model family

Given the forecast spec from Block 2, which model family fits?

Horizon influences model choice, but it doesn't determine it alone — external drivers and seasonality matter just as much.

flowchart TD
  A[Short / medium / long horizon] --> B{External drivers available?}
  B -->|No| C{Clear seasonality?}
  C -->|No| D[ARIMA]
  C -->|Yes| E[SARIMA]
  B -->|Yes| F{Linear and interpretable?}
  F -->|Yes| G[ARIMAX / SARIMAX]
  F -->|No| H{Data scale?}
  H -->|Moderate| I[Tree-based ML]
  H -->|Large sequential data| J[LSTM / deep learning]
For a long horizon, add a side exit: Is structural change likely? → Yes → Scenario/structural modelling and judgement.
Model tendencies by horizon — Short generally favours ARIMA/SARIMA. Medium generally favours SARIMA/SARIMAX when seasonal patterns and drivers matter. Long generally requires structural assumptions, scenarios and judgement. ML and LSTM are not automatically better for longer horizons.
Hotel occupancy has seasonality and a usable external driver (tourist arrivals) with a roughly linear relationship. Which family fits?
ARIMAX / SARIMAX. Seasonality alone would point to SARIMA — but a usable, roughly-linear external driver pushes the choice to SARIMAX, which adds the driver on top of SARIMA's seasonal structure.
Beyond this workshop: when relationships are non-linear or the dataset is large and sequential, tree-based ML or LSTM/deep learning become the relevant branches — not covered here, but worth knowing they exist.
Microlearning 3.2 · Prepare the series

A monthly series has a rising level and an annual repeating pattern. Which transformation(s) should you investigate?

Decide whether the series needs no differencing, regular differencing, seasonal differencing, or both.

Both regular and seasonal differencing — then validate. The rising level needs regular differencing to remove trend; the annual repeating pattern needs seasonal differencing on top of it.
Microlearning 3.3 · Identify AR, MA and seasonal structure

Which diagnostic clue points to an AR(p) process rather than an MA(q) process?

Read ACF/PACF plots with business intuition, not just memorised rules.

ComponentBusiness interpretationDiagnostic clue
ARPersistence or momentumPACF tends to cut off
MATemporary effects of shocksACF tends to cut off
Seasonal ARSeasonal persistencePACF spikes at seasonal lags
Seasonal MASeasonal shocksACF spikes at seasonal lags
ACF decays gradually, PACF cuts off after lag p. A sharp PACF cutoff is the signature of persistence (AR) rather than a temporary shock (MA).
Microlearning 3.4 · Build the candidate model

SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)ₛ — can you match each symbol to what it represents?

Emphasise decisions over memorisation: d, D are transformations for stationarity; p, P are persistence at regular and seasonal lags; q, Q are temporary shocks at regular and seasonal lags; s is the length of the seasonal cycle.

SymbolMeaning
d, DTransformations needed for stationarity
p, PPersistence at regular and seasonal lags
q, QTemporary shocks at regular and seasonal lags
sLength of the seasonal cycle

For ARIMAX/SARIMAX, also decide: which external variables are useful, whether the response is immediate or delayed, whether future driver values will be available, and whether the driver improves out-of-sample forecasting.

For monthly data with a 12-month seasonal cycle, what should s be?
s = 12. s is the number of periods per season — 12 for monthly data with an annual cycle, 4 for quarterly data, and so on.
Microlearning 3.5 · Validate and use the forecast

Model A has the lowest AIC but significant residual autocorrelation. Model B has slightly higher AIC but white-noise residuals and better validation accuracy. Which is more defensible?

Evaluate out-of-sample accuracy, AIC/BIC, residual ACF, the Ljung–Box test, prediction intervals, stability, and business plausibility — not any single number in isolation.

Model B. A lower AIC that leaves autocorrelated residuals means the model hasn't captured all the structure in the data — that unexplained pattern usually costs more in forecast accuracy than the AIC gap saves.
Block output

Use SARIMAX as the candidate model, benchmark it against SARIMA, validate it on a 12-month holdout period, and communicate both the forecast and the prediction interval.

Duration: approximately 60–90 minutes, including guided exercises
