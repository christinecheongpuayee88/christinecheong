Quick quiz2–3 min

SARIMA — Checkpoint 1 topics
Differencing (d) In ARIMA(p,d,q), what does d represent?
  • A. The number of autoregressive terms
  • B. The order of differencing needed to make the series stationary
  • C. The number of moving average terms
  • D. The number of seasonal periods

Why: d counts how many times you difference the series to remove trend and reach stationarity — it says nothing about AR or MA terms.

AR/MA identification A series' ACF decays gradually while its PACF cuts off sharply after lag 2. What process does this suggest?
  • A. MA(2)
  • B. AR(2)
  • C. ARMA(2,2)
  • D. White noise

Why: Gradual ACF decay with a sharp PACF cutoff at lag p is the signature of an AR(p) process — the reverse pattern (sharp ACF cutoff, gradual PACF decay) points to MA(q) instead.

Seasonal period (s) In SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)s, what does s represent?
  • A. The number of seasonal AR terms
  • B. The number of periods per season (e.g. 12 for monthly data with yearly seasonality)
  • C. The seasonal differencing order
  • D. The total number of observations

Why: s is the seasonal period length — easy to confuse with P (seasonal AR order), but s just tells the model how many steps make up one full seasonal cycle.

Seasonal differencing Why do we need seasonal differencing in addition to regular differencing?
  • A. Regular differencing already removes seasonal patterns
  • B. Seasonal differencing removes the repeating s-period pattern that regular differencing leaves behind
  • C. Seasonal differencing replaces the need for an MA term
  • D. It's only needed when d > 1

Why: Regular differencing removes trend; seasonal differencing (at lag s) removes the repeating seasonal pattern — series with both trend and seasonality typically need both.

Model selection (AIC/BIC) Two candidate SARIMA models both have white-noise residuals. Which should you prefer?
  • A. The one with more parameters, since it fits the data more closely
  • B. The one with the lower AIC/BIC
  • C. The one with the higher R²
  • D. Either — residual whiteness is the only thing that matters

Why: Once residuals are white noise for multiple candidates, AIC/BIC breaks the tie by penalizing unnecessary complexity — lower is better.

Residual diagnostics True or False — a well-fitted SARIMA model should leave residuals that look like white noise.
  • A. True
  • B. False — residuals should show a strong seasonal pattern
  • C. False — residuals should trend upward
  • D. False — residual behavior doesn't matter if AIC is low

Why: If the model captured all the structure in the series, what's left over should be unpredictable — i.e. white noise. Leftover pattern means the model missed something.

ARIMAX — Checkpoint 2 topics
ARIMA vs ARIMAX What's the key difference between ARIMA and ARIMAX?
  • A. ARIMAX can only be used for seasonal data
  • B. ARIMAX adds an exogenous predictor on top of Y's own past
  • C. ARIMAX doesn't require differencing
  • D. ARIMAX is only for classification problems

Why: ARIMA models Y using only Y's own history; ARIMAX adds one or more external (exogenous) variables — like advertising spend — as extra predictors.

Identifying X and Y In the Advertising → Sales ARIMAX example, which variable is X and which is Y?
  • A. X = Sales, Y = Advertising
  • B. X = Advertising spend, Y = Sales
  • C. Both are treated as Y
  • D. Neither — ARIMAX doesn't distinguish X and Y

Why: Y is the variable you're forecasting (Sales); X is the exogenous predictor believed to lead or influence it (Advertising spend).

Lagged exogenous effects Why include lagged versions of the exogenous variable instead of just its current value?
  • A. Lags are required by the software regardless of the data
  • B. The effect of X on Y is often delayed or distributed over time
  • C. Lagged values are always more accurate than current values
  • D. It removes the need for differencing

Why: Advertising today might not move sales until next week — lagged X terms let the model capture that delayed, distributed effect (a dynamic transfer-function relationship).

Model evaluation (MAPE) True or False — models were compared using MAPE on a held-out test set.
  • A. True
  • B. False — models were compared using training-set R² only
  • C. False — models were compared by residual count
  • D. False — no quantitative comparison was used

Why: MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) on data the model hasn't seen checks whether it actually generalizes, not just fits the training data.

Overfitting / lag selection Adding more advertising lags worsens out-of-sample MAPE. What should you do?
  • A. Add even more lags to compensate
  • B. Drop the less useful lags — more lags isn't automatically better
  • C. Switch to a pure ARIMA model with no exogenous variable
  • D. Ignore out-of-sample MAPE and trust the training fit

Why: Worsening out-of-sample performance despite more lags is a classic overfitting signal — the extra lags are fitting noise, not signal. Prune back to the lags that actually help.
